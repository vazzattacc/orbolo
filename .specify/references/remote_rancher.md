1. You want to keep eating beef. Good.
This is for the ones who want to keep eating beef, the ones who want to make a difference, the
ones who act.

Where does the food on your plate actually come from? You have probably asked yourself that
lately, perhaps standing in a supermarket aisle in front of an organic cut priced like a small luxury.
And behind that question sits a better one: in a few years, will you be able to look at a steak and
know the animal, the land and the people it came from?

And  you  are  probably  already  thinking  it:  my  money,  in  animals,  on  another  continent,  in  a
hands-on business I have never touched. Is it real? Is it safe? Is it greenwashing with hooves?
Those are the right questions, the ones we would ask ourselves, and this document was written
to answer every one of them in the open, with numbers.

We suspect you already know what you want. Food that comes from living soil. Animals that
spend their lives under trees, raised without growth hormones or antibiotic promoters. People
on  the  land  who  are  paid  well  and  trained  better.  Forests  that  stay,  and  grow.  Every
documentary and every strange summer makes that list a little clearer, and it is a good list to
have.

Perhaps you have the other picture too. A horse, a herd, a long horizon under native trees, work
that ends the day tired in the right way. It is a fine picture, and with a job and obligations it
usually ends here, and it stays a dream. That is fine as well, because there is a way in that fits
your life.

The surest way to change how beef is raised is not to argue about it. It is to own some, and have
a direct impact on their lives. That starts a ripple, and this document is about what the outcomes
of one small decision can be.

And let us be clear from the start: this is not for everyone. It is not for you if you may need this
money back on short notice, if you want a guaranteed return, if a dry year on the other side of
the world would keep you awake at night, or if you would rather hold a paper certificate than
own a living animal. It is for the ones who want to own something real, can let it work for years,
and enjoy knowing exactly where it stands, in every sense.

2. The offer: one truck, 40 cows
Start a ripple you can name.

Here is what you decide. 40 pregnant cows in the Paraguayan Chaco, registered and held for
you,  standing  on  your  balance  sheet,  kept  by  cowboys  on  horseback  under  algarrobo  and
quebracho, and raised without growth hormones or antibiotic promoters; in Paraguay that is
not a marketing claim, it is the law. Capital of US$ 85,800, once, all-in: the cows, their calves to
be,  transport,  registration,  tags  and  2  years  of  keep.  On  the  ground  that  money  moves  in
guaraníes: about ₲498,000,000 at the August 2026 rate of ~5,800 Gs to the dollar. For sanity
and reference, every calculation in this document stays in USD. The all-in price itself is set per
muster season, at that season's cattle prices and exchange rates, and locked at signing; markets
adapt, and the dollar alone has lost about 13% in the past 9 months.

Grass-finished, not just grass-fed: the animals live their whole lives on pasture and are finished
on it, which is not the same as grass-fed cattle that end their days in a feedlot. From there we
are working towards organic. The aspiration is to be competitive in the conventional market first
and to profit from certifications later, as they come; getting there brings no significant additional
cost, because the way the herd is kept already meets most of what the standards ask. What it
does take is time and paperwork: the estancias vary, and certified or pre-certified farms are not
a common thing in the Chaco, so certification is a road we walk, not a badge we start with.

Once a year comes the muster: the roundup. The whole herd is gathered into the pens, counted,
scanned and sorted; the calves are weaned, everything that is sold that year is sold, and the
daughters  who  stay  are  chosen.  One  working  week  in which  the  year's  biology  becomes the
year's money. It is the herd's payday, and yours.

What comes back: a preferred 5.5% on your capital, $4,720 a year, first in line after the herd
itself is fed and cared for; then 45% of the surplus. At today's prices that is about $8,250 a year.
About $800 of it stays in the paddock from the first muster on: 1 daughter is set aside each year
as a future mother instead of being sold. Biology sets her pace, she calves for the first time in
Year 3, and from then on your herd grows by 1 mother every year: 44 by Year 6, 50 by Year 12.
The herd does not just pay you, it gets better at paying you: the $8,250 minus that $800 leaves
about $7,450 in cash each year at today's prices, plus a new cow you own, and a preferred return
that stands on more legs every year.

One thing this is not: an alternative to an index fund. As a pure financial instrument, a liquid fund
will always be easier to buy and easier to sell. As what it actually is, this is the cheapest way on
earth to be a rancher. Compare it not with a fund but with a holiday flat or a boat: assets that
yield nothing, demand everything, and are bought every day for what they let their owner be.
This one pays you, grows by itself and plants a forest. And it quietly does one more job: it places
part of your capital outside Europe, in a real asset on real land in South America, a store of value
with its own weather and its own market.

3. Meet the guide. Meet Verdant 1.
Who is behind this, what has been tried, and what we do not yet know.

The concept itself is older than we are. Paraguayan ranchers have long practised capitalización
de ganado: one party owns the cattle, another owns the land and does the work, and the gain,
the calves and the weight the animals put on, is shared between them. The Remote Rancher
takes that tradition, adds ownership that stands on your balance sheet, tags and a clear set of
rules, and makes it accessible to anyone who wants a direct hand in how their food is produced.
We are the guide. You are the one who moved.

Orbolo's  mission  is  to  balance  natural  and  human  ecosystems  through  projects  that  are
profitable for everyone on the land. Florian Senger-Weiss, founder of Orbolo, has been scouting
Paraguay  since  2022,  has  invested  in  the  country,  where  his  family  lives,  and  before  that
supported farmers in Austria, Germany and Spain in the change towards resilient agroecology.
He runs the first pilot iteration of this modernized capitalización model and called it Verdant,
because  it  supports  resilient,  lush,  green  nature.  Verdant  1:  almost  500  animals  on  our  own
estancia, El Tapir. It has not yet closed a full cycle and it runs without the additional GPS tracking
tags.  It  has  already  taught  us  the  kind  of  thing  no  spreadsheet  holds:  processes,  worst-case
scenarios, fire-testing the excel and the battle plan. Verdant 2 is those lessons written into a
sharper  model:  more  traceability  through  tags  on  every  animal,  adjustments  to  the
implementation  and  distribution  of  every  dollar,  and  greater  focus  on  impact  to  the  direct
stakeholders, nature included.

The Chaco

The Gran Chaco is South America's second-largest wilderness after the Amazon: roughly  100
million hectares of dry forest across Paraguay, Argentina, Bolivia and a corner of Brazil, and one
of the least inhabited places on earth. Days of driving, a handful of towns, algarrobo, quebracho
and palo santo, and the animals that belong to them: the jaguar, the tapir, the giant anteater,
hundreds of bird species. It is also a frontier under pressure, with forest cleared for farmland
every year. That is exactly why a model that pays landowners to keep the forest standing belongs
here, and nowhere better.

Why Paraguay

Because cattle are its culture. Introduced by the settler Juan de Salazar in the 1550s, Paraguay
has raised beef for more than 400 years and today exports it to more than 40 countries; the
know-how, the vets, the auction houses and the cowboys are all here. Because it is stable: a
small, open economy with low public debt, low taxes and a steady currency, and a great deal of
room to grow.

Because the country is ahead on the things that matter to this model: almost all of its electricity
is  renewable,  from  the  Itaipú  and  Yacyretá  dams,  and  the  state  is  encouraging  sustainable
ranching rather than merely tolerating it, with a national sustainable-beef standard  from the
Ministerio  del  Ambiente  y  Desarrollo  Sostenible  del  Paraguay  (MADES)  and  a  regulation  on
deforestation that keeps a share of the forest standing. There are many more ways in which the
line between the untamed wild and the productive tamed can come together and build more
resilience.

4. Walk a year in the cowboy's boots
Under the algarrobos: cattle, horses and tereré.

Act one: the truck arrives

Here is your first morning. It is still dark, and a cattle truck is coming up the track at the estancia.
Yimmy, Diego and the other cowboys are already at the pens. They receive your 40 cows off the
ramp, count them, and check each one's health. Then the new digital tags go on and are mapped
to the legal tags already in the ear; from now on the team knows at all times where each animal
is and how she is behaving, and you see that picture in your reports. Then the practical work
begins.

The horses are saddled. The trough is filled and checked. And it is time to bring the new arrivals
to  school.  We  manage  our  farms  with  mobile  electric  fences  rather  than  fixed  fencing:  it  is
mounted only where and when it is needed, and it leaves the existing ecosystem and the local
wildlife  almost  untouched.  Diego  spends  the  first  3  days  teaching  the  cows  the  connection
between the thin line and the big fixed fence. Then they are integrated into the large herd. Cattle
are herd animals, and we do our best to mimic their natural movement and behaviour as closely
as we possibly can.

Yes, all of this happens because of the decision you made.

Act two: the long middle

This is the part that asks for patience. The rain comes late. Some afternoons reach 45 °C and the
herd stands in the shade of the algarrobo, eating the pods that fall and the grass that stays green
longer here, because the microclimate between tree and soil holds the moisture in. One morning
Diego, on the paddock rotation, finds jaguar tracks at the trough. He moves the cows closer in
and  puts  the  donkey  in  with  the  herd:  the  most  vigilant  and  the  loudest  of  all,  it  will  alert
everyone if the majestic cat comes nearer.

Around now the pregnancy scan is done and logged against each tag, so your report will show
which cows are carrying. In a dry year, expect fewer calves and a thinner surplus, and expect
the herd itself to hold: it is kept whole by the daughters of better years. The soil under it is slowly
getting better. This is the middle of your year, long and quiet. All should be tranquilo.

Act three: birth and pregnancy

This is the most delicate week of the entire year. The tags signal the week in which each cow is
most likely to give birth. She is brought into the paddocks around the house for close monitoring,
for help at the birth if it is needed, and to make sure the calf is strong and well. Then the two of
them rejoin the herd. Usually a few weeks later the cow is in heat again, and the tag signals that
as well. This is when we let the bulls in with the cows; in the wild, too, they live apart for most

from. That is the ripple you started, and it asked you to decide, not to become a cowboy. And
we are working on closing that last link, so that before long you can savour meat from your own
stock.

What happens with the proceeds

The tradition from §3 becomes an explicit payout order here, in writing. The cows get paid first.
Then you. Then the surplus is shared.

Sales. The herd earns money in one way only: calves are weaned and sold, the “weaners”, and
the cows that came up empty are sold as well-fed adults, the “cull cows”. About 22 weaners at
~$850 and ~8 cull cows at ~$1,300: roughly $29,100 a year for one herd.

Tier 0, the herd's keep and the reserve. Rent, which is not dead money: the grass grows on the
rented land, so rent is the herd's feed bill, an input that comes back as weight and calves. Then
cowboys'  wages,  animal  health,  tags  and  their  subscription,  bulls  and  artificial  insemination
service,  freight  and  SENACSA  on  sales,  the  daughters  growing  into  next  year's  herd,  and  the
reserve contribution (capped at $50 per animal, rolling, paid out at cycle end if unused). About
$15,900 in total; the full break-up is in Appendix A.

Tier  1,  your  preferred return and  the  base  expenses.  Your  preferred  5.5%  on  the  full  initial
capital of $85,800, $4,720 a year, and beside it Orbolo's base expenses of 2% of sales, about
$580, which is what it costs to run the registry work, reporting and the programme. Each year
stands on its own. The rate is set deliberately and balanced: low enough that even a lean year
covers it, high enough to mean something.

6. Scenario calculations
Every kind of year, with its effect on your return in one line.

This is ranching in the Chaco, and we will not dress it up. It has its obvious risks when compared
to a government bond. At the same time it is a particular kind of resource allocation: your capital
is not a number on a screen. It stands in a paddock, on 4 legs, eating grass, positively impacting

exporting to more than 40 countries, would have to go to zero. We control the management,
the tags, the rules and the reporting; the weather, the market and the biology set the range.

Inflation

Droughts are regular in the Chaco and are built into every model
above, not treated as surprises. Pregnancy dips, culls rise, prices
soften. In a very lean year the preferred may come in below the full
amount; each year stands on its own, everyone shares the lean year,
the reserve absorbs the timing, and 2 in a row means we sit down
together.

Every ±$50 per weaner moves sales by about ±$1,100 a year and your
cash by about ±$500.

All figures in this document are in today's USD. Costs and cattle prices
have historically moved together, so inflation largely passes through;
the preferred, fixed in dollars, is easier to cover each inflationary year,
and livestock is itself a store of value. Should costs outrun prices for a
stretch (costs +5% a year against prices +2.5%), the return compresses
towards 8 to 9% and the exit values barely move.

10. Why the grass gets better every year
Voisin, Savory, silvopasture and CREA: the science behind the herd, in the time it takes to drink
a tereré.

Grass has a rhythm

Cattle,  moved  well,  build  soil.  In  the  1950s  the  French  farmer  and  biochemist  André  Voisin
worked out that grass has a rhythm: it wants to be grazed hard and briefly, then left alone long
enough to regrow fully before it is grazed again. He called this rational grazing. Allan Savory,
working in African rangeland, took it further with holistic planned grazing, treating the density
and movement of a herd as tools. Move the animals often, in a tight group, and they trample
plant matter into the soil, spread manure evenly, and leave before they can bite the regrowth.
The pasture recovers, roots go deeper, and the ground begins to store water and carbon.

Under the surface the mechanism is simple. A grass plant keeps roughly as much of itself below
ground as above. Grazed briefly and rested long, it sheds and regrows roots with each cycle, and
the shed roots feed the fungi, bacteria and earthworms that turn plant carbon into stable soil
organic matter. Each additional percentage point of organic matter holds roughly 150,000 more
litres of water per hectare; the soil darkens, crumbles instead of crusting, and carries the herd
through a dry January that would have stopped a bare paddock. Long-term grazing trials in North
America  that  compare  this  kind  of  adaptive  multi-paddock  grazing  with  continuous  grazing
(Teague and colleagues in Texas, Stanley and Rowntree in Michigan) consistently find more soil
carbon, more water held and more forage per hectare under the planned system, and in several
of them the pasture takes up more carbon than the cattle emit.

Trees and cattle

Silvopasture adds the third layer: trees, grass and animals on the same land. In the Chaco this
means keeping roughly 150 native trees on every hectare of pasture and leaving the other half
of the block as forest. At 45 °C in January, canopy is a working tool. Cattle in shade eat more,
drink less, and conceive more reliably. Algarrobo pods feed the herd through the dry months
when the grass has stopped. The algarrobo is a legume: its roots fix nitrogen and fertilise the
grass around it, its pods are rich in sugar and protein, and its canopy keeps the soil underneath
cooler and moister, which is why the grass under it stays green longer. The quebracho and the
palo santo hold the deep soil together and shelter the wildlife that belongs here: the jaguar, the
tapir that gave our estancia its name, the giant anteater, hundreds of bird species.

Methane and CO₂

Yes, cattle emit methane and other gases. So do you. Although 2 things are usually left out when
that is all that is said.

1. The short cycle. Methane from cattle is part of a short cycle: the carbon in it was in the grass
a few months before, and after about a decade in the air it returns to CO₂ and to grass again, so
a herd of stable size adds little new warming, while fossil carbon accumulates for centuries.

2. Management sets the range. The emissions of a herd depend enormously on how it is kept.
Grass-fed, grass-finished cattle in silvopasture, on soil that is gaining organic matter, sit at one
end of the range; a feedlot on cleared land sits at the other.

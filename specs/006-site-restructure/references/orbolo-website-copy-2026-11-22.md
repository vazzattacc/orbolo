FAQ (accordion in four groups, with FAQ schema)
About Orbolo

 faq.about[0] Is Orbolo an NGO or a business? A business. We work with non-profits on
purpose: they keep us honest on impact, and we bring the business discipline.

 faq.about[1] How does Orbolo make money? It depends on what we do for you. In
Markets we work like a trader, buying and selling. For herds and estancias we charge fees
for coordinating and managing them, and in some models a share of the sales. The details
are in the data room.

 faq.about[2] Is it only about cattle? Cattle come first, because they’re the catalyst.
Honey, buffalo, native forestry and others are on the way.

 faq.about[3] Who checks what you claim? Every animal carries an official ear tag
registered with SENACSA, Paraguay’s animal-health authority. We’re members of the
Roundtable for Sustainable Beef Paraguay. And you’re always welcome to come and see
for yourself.

Investing

 faq.invest[0] How do I know my cattle are real? Each one carries her official ear tag
and is listed in your contract. Come and count them.

 faq.invest[1] What exactly do I own? Specific animals, identified by ear tag, held for
you by our Paraguayan subsidiary under an agreement that makes them yours, not the
company’s.

 faq.invest[2] What returns can I expect? That depends on the model and the market.
In the data room we show realistic scenarios, bad years included.

 faq.invest[3] Which currency do I invest in? Paraguay runs on two. The US dollar
carries most land and cattle deals, while daily costs run in guaraníes. We explain what that
means for your model in our call.

 faq.invest[4] What is the minimum? It depends on the round. Early rounds can start
smaller; larger ones are structured more formally.

 faq.invest[5] Can I get my capital out early? It depends on the asset. Cattle are the
more liquid one: if necessary, we can arrange a quick sale, though it comes at a cost. Land
takes longer to sell.

 faq.invest[6] Where are my cattle sold? Into Paraguay’s cattle market, at market
prices, to ranchers, meat packers and auctions.

Market access

 faq.buy[0] Do you sell to households? No. We work business to business, in container-
sized orders.

 faq.buy[1] Can you supply deforestation-free beef for the EU? For each lot, we show
what we can document, including deforestation-free status where it can be proven.

 faq.buy[2] How long does delivery take, and which certifications can you offer? It
depends on the product, the plant and the destination. Tell us what you need, and we’ll
confirm both with your quote.

Visiting

 faq.visit[0] Can I visit? Yes, and we’d love that. We start with a call; then the invitation
is ours, the travel is yours, and we help you plan it.

Chapter 1 – How it began
 ch1.body

Orbolo started small, in Europe, with a simple idea: people want to know where their food
comes from, and want their money to do something good. The question was where to
prove it. The answer was a place with grass, space and a long cattle tradition. Paraguay.

Chapter 2 – The founder (portrait photo)
 ch2.body

Florian Senger-Weiss is Austrian, but Paraguay runs in the family. In 1889, his great-
granduncle Carlos Pfannl left Vienna for Paraguay and became one of the pioneers of
modern cattle ranching here. He founded a land company and a cattle company, and today
a town, a cooperative and an agricultural school still carry his name. More than a century
later, Florian splits his time between both countries: conferences and buyers in Europe,
then back to the ranch, boots in the dust.

 ch2.quote

“Every generation farmed with what it knew. We want to farm with what we know now,
and leave the land better for the next one.” – Florian Senger-Weiss

Chapter 3 – The Chaco, split in two (photo: forest edge meeting pasture)
 ch3.body

The Gran Chaco is South America’s second-largest forest, hot, dry and astonishingly alive.
Jaguars, giant anteaters, the tapir our showroom is named after. For a century it has also
been a frontier. The Mennonite settlers who arrived here did an amazing job: they built
roads, dairies and cooperatives where there was almost nothing. That chapter fed a
country. The next one has a different question to answer: how do we keep producing
without losing the forest?

 ch3.amazon

More and more evidence suggests the Chaco’s rain depends on the Amazon, and that
what happens to one forest shapes the other. That’s why we look at both.

Chapter 4 – What we’re building now (photo: team and cattle)
 ch4.body

Our first round of Remote Ranchers has completed its first full cycle. Not without
complications, but successfully. Round two is open. Around it, a network is growing:
estancias we manage, producers who meet our standards, buyers who want to know
where their beef comes from. Every animal is traceable. And at El Tapir, our showroom and
testing ground, we try every idea before we roll it out, always improving.

Words we use (folded glossary)
 glossary.items

Sustainability – A forester’s word, first used by Hans Carl von Carlowitz in 1713: never take
more than grows back. · Regenerative – Farming that leaves soil, water and wildlife better
than before. Not a judgement on earlier generations, who farmed with what they knew. ·
Holistic grazing – Moving cattle in a planned way so grass can rest and recover, building on
the work of Allan Savory and André Voisin. · Silvopasture – Cattle grazing under native
trees, which give shade, shelter and habitat. · Traceability – Knowing where each animal
was born, raised and sold. · Deforestation and reforestation – Losing forest, and bringing it
back. We stand against the first and work towards the second.
